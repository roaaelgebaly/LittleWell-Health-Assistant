# Glossary

Apnoea: Temporary cessation of breathing, especially during sleep.
Asthma: A heterogeneous disease, usually characterized by chronic airway inflammation and episodes of
airway narrowing and obstruction causing breathing difficulties.
Bilevel positive airway pressure: A type of non-invasive ventilation that provides two levels of pressure:
higher during inhalation and lower during exhalation.
Bronchiolitis: An acute viral infection of the lower respiratory tract that typically affects infants and
children aged less than 2 years.
Continuous positive airway pressure: A treatment method that uses mild air pressure to keep the
airways open.
Cyanosis: A bluish discolouration of the skin due to low oxygen levels in the blood.
Humidified high-flow nasal cannula: A method for delivery of up to 100% humidified and heated oxygen
at high flow rates of up to 60 litres per minute.
Hypertonic saline: A highly concentrated saline water solution used in nebulization to help relieve
wheezing and breathing difficulty.
Hypoxaemia: Low levels of oxygen in the blood.
Moderate paediatric asthma exacerbation: A sudden increase in asthma symptoms, associated with:
peak expiratory flow >50% best or predicted, increased respiratory rate (<30/min), increased heart rate
(100–120/min), able to complete sentences in one breath and oxygen saturation (on air) 90–95%. These
parameters are applicable to children aged ≥6 years.1
Nebulization: A method of administering medication in the form of a mist inhaled into the lungs.
Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation: A method of oxygen delivery of positive pressure ventilation
via a tight-fitting mask that covers the nose or both the nose and mouth without the need for endotracheal
intubation.
Non-invasive ventilation: A method of providing ventilatory support without the need for endotracheal
intubation.
Peripheral capillary oxygen saturation: A measure of the oxygen saturation level in the blood.
Prodrome: An early symptom indicating the onset of a disease.
Respiratory distress: Difficulty in breathing, often characterized by rapid breathing and use of accessory
muscles.
Respiratory syncytial virus: A common virus that causes bronchiolitis and other respiratory infections in
young children.

### Recommendations for the management of asthma in children and adolescents


### Additional treatment to standard first-line therapy for


### paediatric asthma exacerbations

Recommendation 1a: Nebulized ipratropium bromide should be added to standard first-line therapy
with inhaled short-acting beta2 agonists (SABA) and systemic corticosteroids for severe paediatric asthma
exacerbations in health facilities.
Recommendation 1b: Nebulized ipratropium bromide should be added for moderate paediatric asthma
exacerbations not responsive to initial standard first-line therapy with inhaled SABA and systemic
corticosteroids in health facilities.
Strong recommendation, low-certainty evidence.
Remarks
•
Ipratropium bromide should be given in addition to standard inhaled or nebulized SABA and systemic
corticosteroids in cases where children are not responding or when the severity of the exacerbation is
extreme and impending collapse is likely.
•
It is essential that first-level health care providers are trained and competent to assess the severity of
an acute asthma exacerbation, start first-line therapy appropriately and monitor response.
•
The results of the systematic review favoured the use of nebulized ipratropium bromide but could not
confirm similar effects with administration via metered-dose inhaler (MDI), mask and spacer.

### Second-line therapy options for paediatric asthma


### exacerbations

second-line therapy for severe paediatric asthma exacerbations, and for moderate exacerbations not
responsive to standard first-line therapy with inhaled/nebulized medicines (SABA and ipratropium) and
systemic corticosteroids in health care facilities.
Strong recommendation, low-certainty evidence.

### supporting a probable diagnosis of bronchiolitis in an infant or


### child

Good practice statement 1: In infants and children aged less than 2 years presenting with cough and

•

•
During a respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) outbreak, a higher index of suspicion should be maintained

•
A comprehensive history should also elicit presence of any risk factors and comorbidities, such as
prematurity, children who are immunocompromized, and children with asthma or reactive airway
disease, chronic lung disease, chronic neurological conditions or congenital heart conditions, and
breastfeeding status.

### Guiding principle for assessing infants and children with


### suspected bronchiolitis for hospital admission

Good practice statement 2: In infants and children aged less than 2 years presenting with suspected
bronchiolitis, clinicians should perform a comprehensive assessment for presence of risk factors and signs
of severe disease.
Indications for admissions include: Signs of severe disease, which include IMCI general danger signs,3
high temperature of ≥38°C, central cyanosis or peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2) <90%, severe
respiratory distress, apnoea or history of apnoea, and presence of risk factors for severe disease which
include comorbidities,a prematurity and age <3 months.
Remarks
•
The indications for admission found in the review are mostly similar to the current WHO guideline
recommendations for the admission of severe pneumonia (1).
•
Clinicians should avoid unwarranted admissions that may lead to exposure to nosocomial infections
and avoidable hospital care costs.
•
Local context should be taken into consideration, particularly the availability of follow-up or its
accessibility, including travelling distances for patients, for example.
•
The temperature threshold of ≥38°C in cases of suspected bronchiolitis is significant and might be
justification for admission or close observation.
•
Comorbidities to consider in the history include: congenital cardiac lesions, chronic lung disease,
cerebral palsy and neurological conditions.

### Nebulized hypertonic (3%) saline versus no hypertonic saline

Recommendation 3: In infants and children aged less than 2 years presenting or hospitalized with

•
The majority of studies used a comparison with normal saline which may underestimate the effect of

•
A trial of nebulized hypertonic saline in an individual patient may aid in clearance of secretions, but

Health facilities experience overcrowding during peak RSV season, making any reduction in the length

often require co-administration with bronchodilators.

1. Introduction

<!-- page:20 -->
Respiratory illness is a leading cause of morbidity among children globally, with both acute and chronic
conditions contributing substantially to outpatient visits and hospital admissions throughout infancy,
childhood and adolescence (4). Among the diverse clinical presentations of paediatric respiratory disease,
wheeze is one of the most frequent and clinically important. Wheeze, defined as a high-pitched, musical
sound produced by airflow through narrowed lower airways during expiration, is a common manifestation
of multiple paediatric respiratory disorders.
In children aged less than 2 years, wheeze most commonly occurs in the context of acute viral bronchiolitis
or other causes of small-airway obstruction. In older children and adolescents, wheeze is more often
associated with asthma and other chronic airway diseases. Although these conditions differ in their
underlying pathophysiology and natural history, they often present with similar clinical features, including
wheeze, tachypnoea, chest indrawing and varying degrees of hypoxaemia, particularly during acute
episodes.
Bronchiolitis typically affects infants in the first 2 years of life, with the highest incidence occurring in the
first year (5). It is characterized by acute inflammation of the small airways, most often triggered by viral
infection, leading to airway obstruction, increased work of breathing and impaired gas exchange. Asthma,
in contrast, is a chronic inflammatory airway disease that manifests through recurrent episodes of wheeze,
cough, chest tightness and breathlessness, with symptoms that vary in severity and over time.
Because bronchiolitis and asthma share overlapping clinical features especially wheeze and respiratory
distress, distinguishing between them in routine clinical practice can be challenging, particularly in younger
children and in settings with limited diagnostic capacity. These overlaps are most evident during acute
presentations, when rapid decisions about treatment and referral must be made.
This guideline addresses two of the most common causes of wheeze and acute respiratory illness in
childhood: asthma and bronchiolitis; and provides evidence-based recommendations to support accurate
assessment, appropriate treatment and effective management across the age spectrum from infancy to
adolescence.

## 1.1 Purpose and rationale

1.1.1 Asthma
Asthma control is frequently suboptimal, particularly in low-resource settings (6). Uncontrolled asthma
symptoms lead to school absence and negatively affect children’s educational and social opportunities (6),
while emergency health care use and hospitalization impose substantial costs on families, health systems
and the wider economy. In addition to the burden measured in disability-adjusted life  years, asthma
contributes significantly to avoidable morbidity and premature mortality (8). To make progress towards
Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target 3.4, which calls for a one third reduction in premature mortality
from noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), it is essential to strengthen both acute and long-term asthma
care, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where most asthma-related deaths occur (9). The
importance of effective acute and chronic asthma treatment as priority interventions to reduce premature
mortality has been underscored by initiatives such as NCD Countdown 2030 (10).
Current WHO guidance on asthma management is provided in the Pocket book of hospital care for
children (1), which focuses primarily on acute care, and in the WHO Package of Essential Noncommunicable
Disease (PEN) interventions for primary health care (11). However, there is a need to ensure that these
recommendations reflect the current evidence base and are aligned with the WHO Model List of Essential
Medicines for Children (EMLc), particularly in relation to medicines and treatment strategies for children
and adolescents (12).

1.1.2 Bronchiolitis
Bronchiolitis is a common paediatric illness affecting children aged less than 2 years and is one of the
leading causes of hospital admission in infancy. It is responsible for substantial increases in health care
utilization, including oxygen use, respiratory support and inpatient care, resulting in significant medical
costs and pressure on health systems. In LMICs, bronchiolitis contributes to child mortality through
hypoxaemia, respiratory failure and complications of severe illness. To achieve SDG target 3.2, which aims
to end preventable deaths of neonates and children aged less than 5 years, it is essential to improve the
quality and consistency of bronchiolitis care, particularly in settings with limited resources.
At present, there are no stand-alone WHO guidelines dedicated specifically to the management of
bronchiolitis, although the condition is addressed within the Pocket book of hospital care for children (1).
There is also rapidly evolving evidence regarding key aspects of bronchiolitis management, including
the role of nebulized hypertonic saline, high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy and continuous
positive airway pressure (CPAP) in the escalation of care and prevention of respiratory failure (13). This
evolving evidence base has not yet been clearly or consistently translated into global guidance. There is
therefore a need to ensure that recommendations for bronchiolitis reflect current best evidence and are
aligned with the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines for Children (12) and the Interagency list of medical
devices for essential interventions for reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health (14).
Asthma and bronchiolitis account for a large proportion of paediatric respiratory morbidity, health care
utilization and preventable mortality. Both conditions are prevalent and place substantial demands on
health systems, and require timely, evidence-based management to improve outcomes. A WHO guideline
that provides up-to-date, aligned, and context-appropriate recommendations for the management of
asthma and bronchiolitis is therefore essential to support countries in achieving their child health and
NCD targets under the SDGs.

2. Process and
methods

<!-- page:23 -->
The guidelines for the management of asthma in children and adolescents, and bronchiolitis in infants
and young children were developed in accordance with the principles and procedures outlined in the WHO
handbook for guideline development (15). The guideline development process followed WHO’s standard
steps, which include: (i) defining the general scope of the recommendations; (ii) identifying contributors and
establishing the Guideline Development Group (GDG); (iii) formulating key questions and outcomes using
the population, intervention, comparison and outcome (PICO) format; (iv) conducting systematic evidence
reviews; (v) assessing the certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment,
Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach; (vi) formulating recommendations and drafting of the
guideline narrative; (vii) reviewing and approving the recommendations through the GDG; (viii) conducting
external peer review; (ix) submitting the guideline for review and approval by WHO’s Guidelines Review
Committee; and (x) finalizing and disseminating the final recommendations.

## 2.1 Contributors

A diverse group of contributors participated in the guideline development process, including clinical
experts, methodologists, health system specialists and representatives from WHO headquarters and
its regional and country offices. All contributors were selected based on their expertise and experience,
and their roles were clearly defined in line with WHO policies to ensure effectiveness, transparency and
minimize bias (Annex 1).
The WHO Steering Group comprised members from relevant technical units at WHO headquarters and
WHO’s regional and country offices. The WHO Steering Group provided technical oversight, coordinated
the process and supported project management. This Group also established the general scope of the
guideline, drafted potential key questions and identified and selected the GDG members.
The GDG included members from all WHO regions and a range of professional backgrounds. Their expertise
covered the clinical management of asthma and bronchiolitis, as well as health systems, programme
implementation and patient care. The GDG was supported by a guideline methodologist, who ensured
methodological rigour throughout the process.
Systematic review teams were commissioned to synthesize the evidence for each key question. An External
Review Group, composed of technical and implementation experts, reviewed the final draft of the guideline
and provided feedback on content and presentation.

## 2.2 Management of conflict of interest

In accordance with the WHO handbook for guideline development (15), all individuals involved in the guideline
development process, including members of the GDG, external reviewers and contributors, were required
to disclose any potential conflicts of interest. These disclosures were reviewed by WHO staff and assessed
for relevance and severity. Where a conflict of interest was identified, appropriate measures were taken
to manage it, including exclusion from specific discussions or decisions, or full recusal from participation.
The objective was to ensure transparency, maintain the integrity of the guideline development process and
safeguard against undue influence. All conflict-of-interest declarations and management decisions were
documented and are available upon request. Only individuals with no significant conflicts of interest were
formally invited to join the GDG or External Review Group. Further details are provided in Annex 1.

## 2.3 Evidence retrieval and synthesis

At the initial two virtual GDG meetings, the GDG established the scope of the guideline; the key questions,
using the PICO format; and identified key outcomes. Systematic reviews were commissioned to address
each of the prioritized PICO questions (see annexes 2 and 3). These reviews adhered to Cochrane methods
and standards and were conducted by independent review teams (16). The GRADE approach was used
to assess the certainty of evidence for each critical and important outcome (17). Detailed methods are
described in the respective systematic reviews (Web Annex (18)).

## 2.4 Formulation of recommendations

The GDG meet virtually and in person between July 2023 and February 2024 to review the evidence and
formulate recommendations. The meetings were chaired by a GDG member and facilitated by the guideline
methodologist. The GDG used an explicit evidence-to-decision framework to guide discussions and ensure
transparency in decision-making.
Recommendations were based on a balance of benefits and harms, the certainty of evidence, values and
preferences, acceptability, feasibility, resource use and equity considerations. Each recommendation was
classified as either strong or conditional, with:
•
a strong recommendation indicating high confidence that the desirable effects outweigh the
undesirable effects; and
•
a conditional recommendation reflecting less certainty, acknowledging that the balance of desirable
effects may vary on context or stakeholder values. The choices of individuals and policy or decision-
makers will vary according to the relative values placed on the outcomes, acceptability, resources and
other considerations.
•
Consensus was the primary method for decision-making. All GDG members present had to agree on
the final wording and the strength of each recommendation. If consensus could not be reached, a
vote was planned as the fallback approach. Only GDG members participated in the decision-making
process, although WHO staff, the methodologist and observers contributed to discussions.
•
The GDG also identified research gaps during the formulation process. These reflect the uncertainties
in the evidence base and highlight areas where further research could improve future guideline
development and clinical decision-making. These research gaps are not prioritized or exhaustive but
are intended to inform future research agendas.
•
Following the GDG meeting, the guideline writer drafted the narrative sections, which were reviewed
and approved by the GDG, and then submitted for external peer review by the External Review Group.
After incorporating the feedback, the final recommendations were reviewed and approved by WHO’s
Guidelines Review Committee and prepared for publication and dissemination.

3. Management of
asthma in children
and adolescents

<!-- page:26 -->
Asthma is a chronic respiratory illness, and one of the main NCDs included in WHO’s Global Action Plan for the
Prevention and Control of Noncommunicable Diseases 2013–2030 (19). Every year, an estimated 426 000 people
die from asthma, with half of these deaths in people aged less than 70 years. Over 95% of these premature
deaths occur in LMICs (4). Asthma is a major cause of morbidity in young people and ranks among the
leading causes of disability-adjusted life years worldwide (20). According to the Global Asthma Network
Phase I study, about one in 20 school-aged children have severe asthma symptoms, with prevalence
varying between and within countries (21).
The Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) defines asthma as “a heterogeneous disease, usually characterized
by chronic airway inflammation. It is defined by the history of respiratory symptoms, such as wheeze,
shortness of breath, chest tightness and cough, that vary over time and in intensity, together with variable
airflow limitation” (22).
Symptoms of asthma are typically worse at night and in the early morning, and are due to airway smooth
muscle constriction, also known as bronchoconstriction, and inflammation of the airway lining. These
symptoms are usually mild and may be controlled with medicine and avoidance of known allergens, but
they can result in potentially life-threatening exacerbations.
Children and adolescents with asthma may experience short-term worsening of their asthma symptoms,
with a progressive increase in wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness and cough. These episodes
are known as acute asthma, attacks, flare-ups or exacerbations. In this document, the term exacerbation
will be used. Acute asthma is commonly triggered by viral respiratory tract infections, exercise, change in
weather and exposure to allergens (such as tree and grass pollen) and irritants (such as tobacco smoke or
air pollution) (22).
Treatment aims to reduce airway smooth muscle constriction using bronchodilators and to reduce
airway inflammation using corticosteroids. Inhaled bronchodilators and inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are
recommended for the long-term asthma management in the WHO package of essential noncommunicable
(PEN) disease interventions for primary health care (11). During an acute episode, people with asthma need
to change their usual treatment; for example, increasing their use of inhaled short-acting bronchodilators
and taking a short course of systemic corticosteroids. More severe cases may require emergency
treatment in a health facility and admission to hospital. However, effective asthma management involves
a holistic approach that addresses both pharmacological and non-pharmacological management, as well
as education and self-management aspects. Working in partnership with children and families is key in
promoting good outcomes. Educating people with asthma on how to take the treatment effectively, how
to avoid triggers, what their modifiable risk factors are, and the actions to take during acute exacerbations
via personalized asthma action plans is essential.

## 3.1 Acute management of asthma

Inhaled bronchodilators are the first step in treating an acute asthma exacerbation. Short-acting beta2
agonists (SABA) should be started as initial therapy at the onset of acute symptoms (cough, wheeze,
difficulty breathing). Children with asthma should have access to a SABA inhaler and a plan of how to use
it when an exacerbation occurs. A mild exacerbation can usually be managed at home if symptoms are
relieved promptly with inhaled SABA.
If symptoms do not subside rapidly, patients are advised to seek medical review to assess the severity of
the exacerbation. Assessment and classification of severity as mild, moderate, severe and life-threatening
asthma depend on clinical signs including heart rate, respiratory rate, work of breathing, ability to talk,
mental state and measuring oxygen saturation and peak expiratory flow (PEF) (if possible). The global and
national guidelines available differ slightly in their classification of asthma exacerbation severity, although
the overall clinical pictures described are broadly consistent (Table 1) (22-25).

## 3.2 Long-term management of asthma

The long-term management of asthma with inhaled bronchodilators and ICS has been standard practice
for decades. However, evidence from large multi-centred RCTs, including mostly adults and older children,
suggest that there should be changes in the way that these medicines are used (22); hence, the need to
review the available evidence for children and adolescents.

## 3.3 Scope and target audience

This guideline reviews the current evidence to inform the recommendations for children and adolescents
aged <19  years diagnosed with asthma. It is not intended to encompass all aspects of the acute
management and the long-term management of asthma, but builds on existing recommendations. The
recommendations on acute management relate to the emergency treatment of an asthma exacerbation
in a health care facility, while the recommendations for long-term management relate to the ongoing
treatment of the chronic condition with inhaled medicines, aiming at controlling symptoms and avoiding
acute exacerbations. Therefore, for this guideline, the recommendations encompass the following topic
areas:
•
additional treatment to standard first-line therapy for paediatric asthma exacerbations;
•
potential second-line therapy options for paediatric asthma exacerbations;
•
preferred second- and third-line therapy for paediatric asthma exacerbations; and
•
combination maintenance and reliever therapy (MART) for the long-term management of asthma.
The recommendations cover the options for inhaled and IV treatment in addition to standard first-line
therapy with inhaled bronchodilators and systemic corticosteroids. They also take into consideration the
need for early intervention with effective treatment to avoid potentially fatal outcomes, particularly in
resource-limited settings where it may not be possible to escalate the level of care provided. The prioritized

## 4.1 Additional treatment to standard first-line therapy for paediatric

4.1.2 Inhaled or nebulized ipratropium bromide
Background
The inhaled anti-cholinergic, ipratropium bromide, is a quaternary ammonium derivative of atropine
which reduces mucosal oedema and secretions and relieves cholinergic bronchomotor tone, resulting in
bronchodilation. It is commonly used for the relief of bronchoconstriction due to limited absorption from the
airways and minimal systemic effects (28). The antagonistic effect of ipratropium bromide on acetylcholine
receptors in the airways has also been proposed to be particularly beneficial in viral infections, which cause
acute asthma exacerbations by modifying these receptors (29). Inhaled ipratropium bromide is typically
administered with an oxygen-driven nebulizer, but a metered-dose inhaler (MDI) with a mask and spacer is
a commonly used alternative in low-resource settings where an oxygen supply is unavailable (30).
Ipratropium bromide is seldom used on its own and a review of six clinical trials in 171 children suggested
that it is inferior to inhaled SABA for the management of acute asthma exacerbations in children aged
older than 2 years (31). A combination of inhaled SABA and ipratropium bromide performed better than
ipratropium bromide alone (treatment failure, odds ratio (OR): 2.65; 95% confidence interval (CI): −1.2 to
5.88) in this population (31). When nebulized ipratropium bromide was added to SABA in a review of 472
children aged 1–18 years hospitalized with acute asthma, no evidence of benefit for length of hospital stay
(mean difference (MD): −0.28 hours; 95% CI: −5.07 to 4.52) or response to therapy was found (32). However,
in a systematic review assessing combination inhalation of ipratropium bromide and SABA for the initial
treatment of acute asthma in 2497 children, the addition of ipratropium bromide to SABA reduced the risk
of hospital admission significantly (relative risk (RR): 0.73; 95% CI: 0.63–0.85) and a trend towards greater
effect with increased severity was observed but did not reach statistical significance (33). No severe adverse
effects were attributed to ipratropium bromide in any of the systematic reviews, confirming its favourable
safety profile.
The evidence supporting ipratropium bromide as an effective and safe bronchodilator, and its use in
combination with SABA inhalations, requires further investigation to define its use as an additional
treatment in children who present with acute asthma exacerbations.

Strong recommendation, low-certainty evidence.
Remarks
•
Ipratropium bromide should be given in addition to standard inhaled or nebulized SABA
and systemic corticosteroids in cases where children are not responding or when the
severity of the exacerbation is extreme and impending collapse is likely.
•
It is essential that first-level health care providers are trained and competent to assess
the severity of an acute asthma exacerbation, start first-line therapy appropriately and
monitor response.
•
The results of the systematic review favoured the use of nebulized ipratropium bromide
but could not confirm similar effects with administration via MDI, mask and spacer.
Justification
The review found, with moderate-certainty evidence, that less hospitalization (relative risk reduction (RRR):
5.2%) is needed when nebulized ipratropium bromide is used in addition to standard first-line therapy.
However, when inhaled ipratropium bromide was compared to placebo, the hospitalization rate was similar
(very low-certainty evidence), but a cost benefit is likely (34-36). The GDG considered that length of hospital
stay may be influenced by various aspects of health care delivery (timing of ward rounds, availability of a
pharmacist) and is not solely dependent on the underlying disease process. There was significant variability
in the methods used to quantify improvements in symptoms, and low to very low evidence that it may
favour ipratropium bromide. Additional literature considered suggests that most providers and caregivers
value a reduction in hospitalization and probable improvement in asthma symptoms over a small risk of
non-severe adverse effects and that the use of ipratropium bromide is cost-effective when used for severe
exacerbations (37).

Summary of evidence
The evidence reviewed included 24 studies of 3238 children aged 18 years and younger with acute asthma
exacerbation, as defined by the study criteria. Four studies were conducted in lower-middle-income
countries (India, Pakistan) (38-41) and three in upper-middle-income countries (Mexico, Thailand, Türkiye)
(42-44). Children were recruited from emergency and outpatient departments and all children received and
failed to improve on standard first-line therapy, including at least three inhalations of SABA and systemic
corticosteroids. The addition of ipratropium bromide to standard first-line therapy was compared to
standard first-line therapy alone or in addition to placebo. Ipratropium bromide was typically administered
as a nebulization within the first hour after presentation, but the dosing varied considerably. The critical
outcomes under investigation included hospitalization, escalation of care (mechanical ventilation or
paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission), length of hospital stay, morbidity (symptom improvement)
and adverse effects.
Compared to placebo, the group receiving nebulized ipratropium bromide had a reduced need for
hospitalization (RR: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.64–0.9, moderate-certainty evidence) (38, 42, 43, 45-49), but there was
no difference in PICU admissions (RR: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.35–2.32, low-certainty evidence) (48, 50) or length of
hospital stay in hours (MD: 1.75; 95% CI: −0.87 to 4.36, high-certainty evidence) (51-54) between groups.
One study included children with moderate asthma exacerbation and compared inhaled ipratropium
bromide administered with an MDI to placebo (RR: 1.09; 95% CI: 0.94–1.27) and did not show a benefit
in hospitalization (very low-quality evidence) (55). Improvements in symptoms were measured using
PEF measurement differences after interventions in seven trials (MD: 8.56; 95% CI: 1.24–15.88, very low-
certainty evidence) (40-42, 47, 53, 56, 57); forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) % predicted in three
trials (MD: 15.64; 95% CI: −7.49 to 38.77, very low-certainty evidence) (40, 56, 58); and clinical asthma score
improvement (MD: −0.38; 95% CI: −0.63 to −0.12, low-certainty evidence) in eight trials (39, 40, 42, 47,
51, 53, 56, 57). Adverse events included nausea, vomiting, headaches, dry mouth, abdominal pain and
conjunctivitis and occurred in both groups (RR: 1.39; 95% CI: 0.95–2.05, moderate-certainty evidence) (38,
43, 45, 46, 49, 52, 55, 58).
Benefits and desirable effects
The addition of nebulized ipratropium bromide to standard acute asthma therapy reduced the hospitalization
(RRR: 5.2%) of children presenting with asthma exacerbation. However, the addition of ipratropium bromide
did not reduce the need for PICU admission (RR: 0.91) or length of hospital stay. The GDG considered that
the length of hospital stay may be influenced by various aspects of health care delivery (timing of ward
rounds, availability of a pharmacist) and is not solely dependent on the underlying disease process. An
improvement in symptoms was evaluated by several proxy measures including a significant improvement
seen in PEF (MD: 8.56) and reduction of clinical asthma severity scores (MD: −0.38), but there was no
meaningful difference in FEV1 % predicted (MD: 15.64) between the groups. These measurements were
considered too heterogeneous to influence the recommendation. The GDG also noted that the greater
effect of ipratropium bromide and SABA synergism with increased asthma exacerbation severity previously
observed was not evaluated by the systematic review but may be relevant.
The GDG judged the desirable effects of ipratropium bromide in addition to standard first-line therapy in
acute asthma exacerbations as moderate (low-certainty evidence).

## 4.2 Second-line therapy options for paediatric asthma exacerbations

4.2.1 IV magnesium sulfate
Background
Asthma is a chronic lung disease characterized by airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness of
airway smooth muscles. There is growing evidence that magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) may have a role in
managing asthma through its dual effect as an anti-inflammatory and bronchodilating agent (64, 65).
Bronchodilatation is attributed to the blockade of calcium channels in the airway smooth muscles and
a reduction in airway excitability. This anti-inflammatory process is due to the eradication of oxygen free
radicles, the stabilization of T-cells and mast cells, and the facilitation of endogenous nitric oxide and
prostaglandin release (64, 66). Additionally, magnesium sulfate acts as a beta-receptor agonist, resulting in
a synergistic effect when administered with salbutamol (67).
Previous research and evidence reviews have shown that administering magnesium sulfate via IV or
inhaled routes can help manage acute exacerbations of asthma. Both routes of administration are fast-
acting and effective, although the IV route has greater bioavailability but with associated with systemic
side-effects (68). Intravenous magnesium sulfate is currently used as adjunctive therapy for severe asthma
exacerbations. Its addition to standard asthma therapy in both children and adults has been shown to
reduce hospitalizations and clinical asthma scores (65, 69). However, it is acknowledged that the evidence
in the literature has provided conflicting results, with studies exploring both the IV and nebulized routes of
administration (70-72). In the most recent review of three RCTs in 816 children aged less than 5 years with
acute asthma exacerbation, nebulized or IV magnesium sulfate did not show any benefit in hospitalizations
or clinical outcomes when compared to standard treatment (73).
Despite the conflicting evidence, several asthma treatment guidelines currently recommend giving
IV magnesium sulfate to children having asthma exacerbations who have not responded well to other
treatments, but it remains unclear whether it is safe and similarly effective for children. The evidence
review of IV magnesium sulfate to date is inconclusive and warrants further investigation.

4.2.2 IV aminophylline
Background
Aminophylline is a methylxanthine bronchodilator composed of theophylline and ethylenediamine.
Theophylline relaxes the smooth muscle of the bronchial airways and pulmonary blood vessels and reduces
airway responsiveness to histamine, methacholine, adenosine and other chemical mediators. Aminophylline
causes non-selective modulation of intracellular calcium release, inhibition of phosphodiesterase
and antagonism of the adenosine receptor (90). Furthermore, aminophylline stimulates endogenous
catecholamine release resulting in inotropic and chronotropic effects on myocardium (91). Aminophylline
has a narrow therapeutic range (10–20 mg/dL) and severe toxicity may occur at higher levels, resulting in
tachycardia, diuresis, hypokalaemia, convulsions and sudden death due to cardiac dysrhythmia (92).
The addition of IV aminophylline to standard first-line therapy with SABA and systemic corticosteroids
remains controversial, and treatment guidelines vary from country to country (22, 93, 94). One meta-analysis
concluded that the addition of IV aminophylline improved lung function within 6 hours of treatment but
reduction in symptoms, number of nebulized SABA doses or length of hospital stay, but was associated with
significant increased risk of nausea and vomiting (95, 96). More recently, in a review of IV aminophylline use
in adults, IV aminophylline had no beneficial effects over standard of care and resulted in a 15% increase
in arrhythmias or palpitations (97).
Internationally, there has been a shift away from using IV aminophylline in acute asthma exacerbations
and experts have cautioned against its continued use (22). Despite this, aminophylline is widely available,
affordable and remains part of the limited repertoire of drugs for the management of acute asthma
exacerbations in remote areas (98).
Based on the current available evidence and practices, there is need to define the role of IV aminophylline
in the management of acute asthma exacerbation.

4.2.3 Subcutaneous adrenaline (epinephrine)
Background
Adrenaline is a non-selective agonist of alpha and beta adrenoceptors that causes bronchodilation through
stimulation of beta2-adrenergic receptors leading to bronchial smooth muscle relaxation, while the alpha
agonist effect reduces airway oedema through the stimulation of alpha receptors (111). Historically,
subcutaneous or IM adrenaline was the mainstay of acute asthma treatment but current asthma guidelines
do not recommend its use except in the context of concomitant anaphylaxis or angio-oedema (22, 111, 112).
However, the administration of IM adrenaline in addition to nebulized selective beta2 agonist for acute
severe or life-threatening asthma remains part of many prehospital emergency guidelines and continues
to be used by some clinicians (112, 113).
A recent systematic review and meta-analysis that evaluated the use of adrenaline administered by any
route, compared to the administration of selective beta2 agonist by any route in adults and children with
acute asthma exacerbation (114). The review found limited evidence that there was no benefit from the
use of adrenaline in addition to selective beta2 agonist in acute asthma exacerbation (114). Subcutaneous
adrenaline did not reduce treatment failure or lead to greater bronchodilation than nebulized selective
beta2 agonist alone.
WHO’s Pocket book of hospital care for children currently advises the use of subcutaneous adrenaline in
severe life-threatening asthma if inhaled or nebulized salbutamol is not available (1). This practice dates
to a time when salbutamol was not widely available. There was need to evaluate the current evidence to
support on the use of subcutaneous adrenaline in acute asthma exacerbations.

4.2.4 IV SABA in management of acute severe asthma exacerbation
Background
The current standard of care for the management of acute asthma exacerbations includes inhaled SABA
and systemic corticosteroids (116). SABA is a beta2-adreneceptor agonist and binds to the beta2 receptor,
thereby activating adenyl cyclase which results in the conversion of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to
cyclic adenosine monophosphate (AMP). This promotes bronchodilation and effective symptom control
in acute asthma exacerbation (22). The penetration of inhaled SABA, especially into the small conducting
airways, may be reduced due to airway inflammation associated with severe acute asthma exacerbations,
resulting in a reduced response. It is hypothesized that IV delivery of SABA to the receptors via the systemic
circulation may improve clinical response (117).
A previous systematic review investigated the addition of IV SABA to inhaled SABA in 104 adults and
children and included three RCTs (118). The outcome measures reported in the trials were heterogeneous
and only one study reported statistically significant shortening in recovery time in the IV SABA (salbutamol)
group (P = 0.03) (117). The IV SABA group was discharged 9.7 hours earlier than the inhaled SABA group (P
< 0.05) (117). In the study conducted in the PICU, there was no benefit in length of admission in the IV SABA
(terbutaline) group (MD: −12.95 hours; 95% CI: −38.74 to 12.84) (119). A subsequent observational study in
120 children found a 44% decrease in mechanical ventilation when IV SABA was used and the duration of
mechanical ventilation was shorter in the group who received IV SABA prior to PICU admission (120).
Based on the limited current favourable evidence from single studies, the GDG judged the importance of
defining the role of IV SABA in the management of acute asthma exacerbation as a priority problem.

4.2.5 Preferred second- and third-line therapy for paediatric asthma exacerbations
Background
Current guidance on appropriate second-line therapy for acute asthma exacerbation in children who do
not respond to standard first-line therapy or those presenting with life-threatening asthma is inconsistent.

magnesium sulfate or a terbutaline bolus given over 5 minutes, followed by a continuous infusion (22).

the GDG judged the importance of defining the role of each IV therapy in the management of acute asthma
exacerbation a priority problem.

## Recommendation 5:

IV magnesium sulfate should be the preferred option for second-line therapy and IV SABA
the preferred option for third-line therapy in severe paediatric asthma exacerbations not

Remarks
•
A pragmatic stepwise approach to second-line therapies would be appropriate, and allow
practitioners to choose the best possible option for their setting.
•

additional IV treatment and improve asthma severity score with no increase in undesirable effects.

critical outcome, but at the cost of a small increase in arrythmia. However, for IV aminophylline there was

vomiting. The GDG agreed that due to contextual differences, resource limitations and a significant cost
difference between therapies, a pragmatic approach to second-line therapies would be appropriate and
allow practitioners to choose the best possible option for their setting.
The GDG agreed on a marginal benefit of IV magnesium sulfate, based on very low-certainty evidence, over
IV SABA and IV aminophylline on balance of risk and benefit. However, a stepwise approach to IV therapy
from magnesium sulfate to SABA and then aminophylline was advised. The GDG therefore recommended
the use of IV magnesium sulfate as the preferred option for second-line therapy over IV SABA (second
choice) and over aminophylline (third choice) in patients with acute severe asthma exacerbations not
responding to standard first-line therapy.
Summary of evidence
The meta-analysis investigated studies of comparative interventions to identify second-line therapeutic
options for acute severe asthma exacerbations. The available studies were analysed in three comparative
groups, including IV aminophylline versus other treatment, IV SABA versus other treatment and IV
magnesium sulfate versus other treatment.

4.2.6 Combination inhaler MART for the long-term management of asthma
Background
The long-term management and control of asthma symptoms, particularly in resource-limited settings, is
largely inadequate, with approximately one in 20 children and adolescents experiencing severe asthma
symptoms (21). Uncontrolled asthma symptoms have a significant impact on daily life, causing school
absence, impacting negatively on education and social opportunities (7). Asthma exacerbations
require medical review, escalation of treatment and possibly hospitalization. Without timely and effective
treatment, severe exacerbations can lead to death.
For patients experiencing frequent symptoms requiring relief with SABA, standard practice is to introduce
regular treatment with daily ICS (134). Regular treatment with ICS is well established to reduce asthma-
related deaths, hospitalizations and exacerbations (135-137). According to the Global Asthma Network
Phase I study, the proportion of children worldwide with severe symptoms taking SABA alone, without
additional ICS, was 44.8%, with significantly higher rates in low- and lower-middle-income countries (6). In
children using SABA monotherapy, the use of three or more canisters per year is associated with a twofold
risk of asthma-related hospitalization or emergency department visit, with each additional canister used
per year associated with an 8–14% increased risk of acute asthma exacerbations (138).
Adherence to daily ICS treatment is considered a challenge, and over the past decade there has been
increasing evidence to support a new model of long-term management, using a combination inhaler
containing both ICS (such as budesonide) and a rapid-onset long-acting beta2 agonist (LABA) (such as
formoterol). Rapid-onset LABA, which acts as quickly as SABA (such as salbutamol or terbutaline), can be
used in a combination inhaler with ICS and taken as needed to relieve asthma symptoms, and also for
regular maintenance therapy in those with more persistent symptoms (22). This has the advantage of
simplifying an inhaler regime and ensuring that LABA therapy is not taken without ICS.
Systematic reviews assessing the use of combination inhalers as reliever or MART have included patients
aged 12 years and older, and report favourable reductions in exacerbations and decreased exposure to
inhaled and systemic steroids (139-141).

Summary of evidence
Only one study was identified which assessed the use of combination inhaler MART in children (144). The
data were extracted, as part of a prospectively planned analysis, from a study that recruited patients aged
4–80 years. Participants were recruited at 41 centres across five upper-middle-income and seven high-
income countries. The three-arm double-blind RCT included 341 children, aged 4–11 years, with at least a
6-month history of asthma, and at least one clinically important exacerbation in the preceding 12 months.
All children had been treated with ICS for at least 3 months.
A MART regimen (budesonide/formoterol 80/4.5 µg once daily maintenance plus additional inhalations
for symptom relief) was compared to budesonide/formoterol 80/4.5 µg once daily for maintenance (fixed
combination) or higher-dose budesonide 320 µg once daily (fixed-dose budesonide) (n = 106). Blinded as-
needed SABA medication (terbutaline 0.4 mg) was provided in both fixed-dose groups. Participants were
followed up for 12 months.
The critical outcomes identified by the GDG reported for this study included overall exacerbation rates,
exacerbations requiring medical intervention (hospitalization or emergency department treatment,
treatment with oral steroids, an increase in ICS and/or additional treatment), adverse events, discontinuation
of treatment and daily inhaled steroid dose. Changes in lung function were considered less important by
the GDG. The methodological quality of this study was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for
randomized trials (145) and the certainty of the evidence was determined through the GRADEpro guideline
development tool (146).
The overall exacerbation rates were 14% (17 out of 118 patients) on the MART regimen compared to 38%
(44 out of 117 patients) on the fixed-dose combination (RR: 0.38; 95% CI: 0.23–0.63) and 26% (28 out of
106 patients) on fixed-dose budesonide (RR: 0.55; 95% CI: 0.32–0.94). The rates of exacerbations requiring
medical intervention were 8% (10 out of 118 patients) for patients on MART compared to 31% (36 out of
117 patients) on the fixed-dose combination (RR: 0.28; 95% CI: 0.14–0.53) and 20% (21 out of 106 patients)
on fixed-dose budesonide (RR: 0.43; 95% CI: 0.21–0.87).
The mean daily ICS dose was 126 µg for those using the MART regime, compared to 80 µg for the fixed-
dose combination and 320 µg for fixed-dose budesonide.
PEF measurements were higher in those receiving MART (mean morning PEF: 255 L/min) compared to
fixed-dose budesonide (mean morning PEF: 238  L/min; P  =  0.002) and fixed-dose combination (mean
morning PEF: 242 L/min; P = 0.22). FEV1 measurements were similar in all groups.
Serious adverse events were uncommon and included asthma exacerbations, fractures and pneumonia.
The rate of serious adverse events was lower among those receiving MART (2 out of 118 patients), compared
to fixed-dose combination (16 out of 117 patients) (RR: 0.11; 95% CI: 0.02–0.48) and fixed-dose budesonide
(5 out of 106 patients) (RR: 0.35; 95% CI: 0.07–1.83). Class-related effects, such as tremor, dysphonia and
tachycardia were rare and evenly distributed between the three groups.
There was no difference in discontinuation of treatment between those receiving MART (2 out of 118
patients) and those in the two other treatment arms (2 out of 223 patients) (RR: 1.89; 95% CI: 0.27–13.25).

5. Management of
bronchiolitis in infants and

<!-- page:56 -->
Bronchiolitis is an acute viral infection of the lower respiratory tract that typically affects infants and
children aged less than 2 years, and is the leading cause of hospitalization in children globally (154). It
is primarily caused by the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), but other viruses including adenovirus,
human metapneumovirus, influenza and parainfluenza contribute significantly (154, 155). Children with
viral bronchiolitis are at increased risk of invasive bacterial infections, including pneumonia, and have a
higher risk of developing asthma and recurrent wheezing in later childhood (156-158). Severe disease is
more common among preterm babies, children younger than 6 months and children with comorbidities,
specifically, the immunocompromised and children with congenital heart disease, neuromuscular disorders
and other chronic neurological conditions, chronic lung disease, and asthma or reactive airway disease
(159-161). Estimates suggest that RSV disease results in more than 30 million cases of lower respiratory
tract infection in children aged less than 5 years annually, with 3.2 million hospitalizations and 200 000
deaths worldwide each year (162). Acute bronchiolitis is also associated with a high economic burden
from the utilization of health care resources, with the highest costs attributable to severe bronchiolitis in
children with comorbidities and prematurity (163).

## 5.1 Clinical presentation

Acute bronchiolitis typically presents with acute onset of upper respiratory prodrome of rhinitis or
congestion, low-grade fever and cough progressing to the symptoms of increasing respiratory distress
(tachypnoea and increased work of breathing) and wheezing (155, 164). Common signs include tachycardia,
tachypnoea, hyperinflated chest, hyperresonant sounds on chest percussion, wheezing and crackles. In
children with severe disease, there may be grunting, marked retractions, cyanosis or lethargy. Apnoea is
common in preterm babies and those aged less than 2 months. The severity of bronchiolitis can vary from
mild symptoms to acute respiratory failure, but most cases are self-limiting and respond to symptomatic
care at home. Complications among the hospitalized may include severe respiratory distress or failure,
pneumothorax, myocardial injury, neutropenia, sepsis, shock and neurological complications (165, 166).

## 5.2 Diagnosis and management

The diagnosis and assessment of acute bronchiolitis is based on history and clinical examination.
Laboratory investigations, chest radiographs and virological studies are not routinely indicated (13, 164).
The majority of children can be managed at home with supportive care on an ambulatory basis. Home
care includes parental education about adequate feeding, nasal saline drops and the gentle suctioning
of nostrils to relieve nasal block, and early care-seeking in case of any worsening symptoms (13, 164). The
current practice for those hospitalized is supportive care, including humidified oxygen therapy, adequate
hydration and feeding, and close monitoring, with no specific interventions of proven benefit despite wide
variability in the acute bronchiolitis care (13, 164, 167). Children with severe respiratory distress may require
invasive mechanical ventilation, which is associated with various complications (ventilator-induced lung
injury, infection, airway trauma, vocal cord dysfunction and need for prolonged sedation) and significant
financial costs (168). However, various non-invasive modes of oxygen delivery are increasingly being used,
such as HFNC, CPAP, non-invasive positive pressure ventilation and bilevel positive airway pressure, which
may obviate the need for invasive mechanical ventilation and the complications related to it (169-173).

## 6.1 Clinical signs and symptoms for the diagnosis of bronchiolitis

Guiding principle for using clinical symptoms and signs in supporting a probable
diagnosis of bronchiolitis in an infant or child
Good practice statement 1: In infants and children aged less than 2 years presenting with
cough and difficulty in breathing, a comprehensive history and assessment of clinical signs
and symptoms should be done. A diagnosis of bronchiolitis can be suspected if they have a
history of a prodrome of an upper respiratory tract infection with rhinitis or coryza and cough,
followed by respiratory distress with a low-grade fever, and any presence of tachypnoea (fast
breathing), chest indrawing and diffuse wheeze or crackles on auscultation.
Remarks
•
Assessment of clinical signs should include the IMCI (3) general danger signs,4 cyanosis,
respiratory rate, looking for chest indrawing, listening for a wheeze or crackles, and
measuring oxygen saturation.
•
Infants with viral bronchiolitis are usually not very ill looking, and many have a prolonged
expiratory phase of breathing associated with wheeze.
•
During an RSV outbreak, a higher index of suspicion should be maintained in the differential
diagnosis for infants presenting with respiratory symptoms.
•
A comprehensive history should also elicit presence of any risk factors and comorbidities,
such as prematurity, children who are immunocompromized, and children with asthma
or reactive airway disease, chronic lung disease, chronic neurological conditions or
congenital heart conditions, and breastfeeding status.

with the appropriate use of antibiotics. All studies were assessed as having high risk of bias owing to study
design, some were retrospective with missing data, or had a low sample size of less than 100, or had
reporting concerns.

6.3.1 Justification
Bronchiolitis is primarily a clinical diagnosis based on history and physical examination findings, including
cough, wheezing, tachypnoea and respiratory distress. In most cases, the diagnosis can be made based on
these clinical features alone without the need for confirmatory imaging studies. Despite the heterogeneity
in the three studies (188-190), the outcomes were consistent that routine chest X-ray (CXR) has no clinical
value in typical bronchiolitis, increases the risk of unnecessary antibiotic use and escalates care costs.
Driven by these outcomes, along with the evidence that imaging is unlikely to change management
outcomes, the GDG decided against routine CXR for suspected bronchiolitis. Routine CXR typically has a
low diagnostic yield in children with bronchiolitis, particularly in those without signs of severe respiratory
distress or complications.
6.3.2 Summary of evidence
The systematic review identified two prospective studies and one retrospective study conducted in the
emergency departments of tertiary hospitals in three high-income countries, with a total of 1194 children
aged less than 2 years with clinically diagnosed bronchiolitis (188-190). All the three studies reported on the
rates of antibiotic therapy in those that received CXR or change in management after CXR, and, in addition,
one prospective cohort study reported on the proportion of CXRs that were inconsistent with clinically
diagnosed bronchiolitis. The infants in the included studies had moderate to severe bronchiolitis, with a
median age of 2.2 months. The studies had mostly low risk of bias, mainly driven study design (188-190).
The findings from the prospective cohort study suggest that 133 children with typical bronchiolitis would
have to undergo radiography to identify one CXR that is suggestive of an alternate diagnosis (190). In
addition, it showed that children were five times more likely to receive antibiotic therapy post CXR compared
to the intended management plan pre CXR. The other prospective study of 697 children evaluated the
relationship between CXR use and antibiotic prescribing. Antibiotic use was significantly higher among
those who received a CXR (31%, 108 out of 355) compared with those who did not (8.4%, 28 out of 342). The
crude OR was 4.9 (95% CI: 3.1–7.8) (189). The retrospective cohort study assessed 227 children hospitalized
for acute bronchiolitis who received a routine CXR and 35 (15.4%) children who received a second CXR
in the event of complications. The routine CXRs showed lung abnormalities, specifically distension and/
or bronchial wall thickening (62.4%), focal opacity (19.9%) or atelectasis (17.7%). Antibiotic treatment was
started in 29 children (12.5%) following use of first CXR (188).
Overall, the three studies suggest that CXR contributed to only partial alteration of treatment of infants
hospitalized for acute bronchiolitis, but increased the risk of unnecessary administration of antibiotics.
There was low-certainty evidence about the additional value of CXR in management of bronchiolitis in
infants aged less than 2 years.
Benefits and desirable effects
The GDG determined that there was trivial benefit from doing routine CXR in all suspected bronchiolitis
cases. There was low-certainty evidence that routine CXR was not useful in confirming the diagnosis of
bronchiolitis, and that it contributed only partially to alteration of treatment and leads to unnecessary
antibiotic use with no clear benefits to patients. The characteristic radiographic findings of bronchiolitis,
such as hyperinflation and peri bronchial cuffing, are nonspecific and do not reliably differentiate
bronchiolitis from other viral respiratory infections. Nevertheless, the GDG observed that CXR may be
beneficial in identifying complications and the promotion of the appropriate use of antibiotics.

## 6.4 Administration of supplemental oxygen through nasal bubble


## CPAP


## No recommendation:

WHO makes no recommendation on the use of nasal bubble CPAP to administer oxygen rather
than standard flow oxygen (SFO) through nasal prongs/face mask/headbox for better clinical
outcomes.
6.4.1 Justification
The GDG observed that the available evidence was based on a single small study, in which the criteria of
the outcomes used had varied definitions that may vary from country to country, and which did not include
any of the critical outcomes of interest. The 1 hour of follow-up to assess the need for the escalation
of treatment and/or change in the respiratory rate as outcomes did not provide sufficient evidence to
make a recommendation. Pulse oximetry would have been a more objective way of measuring clinical
improvement in hypoxia, which was not reported in this single study.
The GDG decided not to make a recommendation because of inadequate evidence and knowledge gaps
requiring further research.
6.4.2 Summary of evidence
Benefits and desirable effects
The systematic review identified only one RCT with a sample size of 72 infants aged less than 12 months
who were randomized to 36 in the bubble CPAP arm and 36 in the standard arm. The primary outcomes
measured after 1 hour were change in respiratory rate, Silverman–Anderson score and Modified Paediatric

The GDG made no comment on any other aspects of the administration of supplemental oxygen through

## 6.5 Humidified high-flow oxygen versus standard low-flow oxygen


## therapy

6.5.1 Background
Oxygen therapy supplementation is a commonly prescribed intervention when caring for hypoxic or
severely ill patients. It often takes the form of standard low-flow oxygen which can deliver up to a maximum
of 6  litres per minute or fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) of approximately 0.45 via nasal cannula or
prongs. In standard low-flow oxygen therapy, FiO2 delivery is directly tied to flow rate, and above 6 litres
per minute may lead to nasal mucosal irritation and drying of the passages. Humidified high-flow nasal
cannula (HHFNC) therapy is an oxygen supply system capable of delivering up to 100% humidified and
heated oxygen at a flow rate of up to 60 litres per minute. The high-flow system ensures delivery of a
fixed concentration of oxygen and the generation of positive end-expiratory pressure, reduces the work of
breathing and clears the nasopharyngeal dead space.
In clinical practice, HHFNC may be used as an alternative to CPAP systems for apnoea of prematurity and
post extubating (194). Some of the reported advantages of HHFNC over CPAP include less trauma to the
nares, simpler application and making it easier to care for the baby as it gives better access to the baby,
including for skin-to-skin contact and feeding (195). Undesired effects include nasal erosion, tiredness
when used over long periods, bradycardia, desaturation or high carbon dioxide, and, in addition, “rain out”
in the circuit can result in lavage and apnoea (196, 197). Studies have shown HHFNC to be safe as an initial
respiratory management, and it is thought to be of benefit for children with bronchiolitis compared with
standard oxygen therapy or nasal CPAP (198).

## Recommendation 2:

In infants and children aged less than 2 years, hospitalized with acute bronchiolitis with
hypoxaemia, HHFNC oxygen is suggested rather than SFO.
Condition recommendation, low certainty of evidence.
Remarks
•
In most cases, children with acute bronchiolitis should be started on standard low-flow
oxygen, which is very often effective in resolving the hypoxaemia.
•
HHFNC oxygen is indicated where, despite standard low-flow oxygen therapy, the infant
has persisting hypoxaemia (SpO2 <90%) or severe respiratory distress.

6.5.2 Justification
The recommendation in favour of HHFNC was driven by the moderate certainty of evidence of benefit for
the resolution of symptoms/signs such as respiratory rate, heart rate and oxygen saturation, which the
GDG considered to be critical outcomes. The GDG also considered the reduction of 0.85 of a day of length
of stay in ICU as a clinically important outcome in a situation of a peak RSV epidemic, when there are often
too many infants that need hospitalization, as this may save the lives of those who are waiting for hospital
beds. Consequently, the GDG issued a conditional recommendation for high-flow oxygen rather than SFO.
However, the GDG also noted that if the patients started on SFO have responded well, they do not need to
be changed to HHFNC.
6.5.3 Summary of evidence
The systematic review identified seven RCTs comparing HHFNC and SFO conducted in six countries in
Africa, Asia and Europe, with a total of 2197 infants and children with moderate to severe bronchiolitis
(198-204). The HHFNC studies titrated the fraction of inspired oxygen used to maintain oxygen saturation
in a pre-specified range, and the SFO used a variety of delivery methods including nasal prongs, face masks
and headbox oxygen. All seven studies reported on length of hospital stay and resolution of symptoms,
five studies on escalation to the ICU (199, 201-204), and only three studies reported on adverse events
(201-203). Although all were RCTs, all studies had some bias driven by lack of masking/blinding to the
intervention, and some had crossovers from the SFO to the HHFNC arms due to treatment failure.
The mean length of stay in hospital was lower in infants that received HHFNC compared to SFO (weighted
mean difference (WMD): −0.40 days; 95% CI: −1.071 to 0.27; low-certainty evidence), but this finding was
not statistically significant. Two studies reported on length of stay in the ICU; the mean length of stay in
the ICU was lower among infants who received HHFNC compared to the standard of care, which was SFO
(WMD: −0.85 days; 95% CI: −2.39 to 0.69); however, this finding was not statistically significant (200, 202). In
five studies, similar findings that there were fewer infants escalated to the ICU in the HHFNC arm than in
the SFO arm (risk ratio (RR): 0.94; 95% CI: −0.57 to −1.53, moderate-certainty evidence), although again this
was not statistically significant (199, 201, 203, 204). HHFNC was associated with higher rates of symptom
resolution compared to standard oxygen therapy (RR: 1.15; 95% CI: 1.08–1.21; four RCTs including 1887
participants). This equates to 117 more children per 1000 achieving treatment success (moderate-certainty
evidence) (199-202).
Seven types of adverse events were reported that were experienced by a total of 19 infants, including
apnoea (two infants on HHFNC, three on SFO), epistaxis (two infants on SFO), inhaled condensation (one
infant on HHFNC), oxygen desaturation (one infant on HHFNC), oxygen tube disconnection (two infants on
SFO), pneumothorax (one infant on HHFNC, one infant on SFO) and transient bradycardia (three infants
on HHFNC, two infants on SFO) (201-203). When “any adverse event” in the two trial arms were compared,
infants that received the HHFNC intervention experienced 57.9% (11 out of 19 infants) adverse events
compared to 42.1% (8 out of 19 infants) among those who were administered SFO (201-203).
Overall, there was moderate certainty of evidence of higher treatment success among those receiving
HHFNC therapy, and moderate certainty of evidence of the length of stay in ICU and escalation to ICU for
further respiratory support, but low certainty of evidence the same length of stay in hospital. There is no
evidence to suggest HHFNC brings about substantially more adverse events than SFO.

## 6.6 Nebulized hypertonic (3%) saline versus no hypertonic saline

6.6.1 Background
Bronchiolitis is caused by a viral infection and often leads to hospitalization due to respiratory distress.
Bronchiolitis occurs because of the inflammation of the lining of the epithelial cells of the small airways in
the lungs causing mucus production, inflammation and cellular necrosis of these cells. It is the inflammation
of these cells that can obstruct the airway and, ultimately, result in wheezing. Treatment primarily focuses
on supportive care, as there are limited effective pharmacological therapies.
Hypertonic saline (a strong or highly concentrated, usually ≥3%, sterile saltwater solution) breathed in as
a fine mist using a nebulizer may help relieve wheezing and breathing difficulty (208). It is thought to work
by reducing airway oedema through osmotic effects, enhancing mucociliary clearance and rehydrating
airway surfaces (209). However, studies do not consistently provide evidence for a clear long-term benefit
of using nebulized hypertonic saline.

## Recommendation 3:

In infants and children aged less than 2 years presenting or hospitalized with bronchiolitis, a
trial of nebulized hypertonic saline (3%) is suggested rather than no nebulization.
Conditional recommendation, low certainty of evidence.
Remarks
•
The majority of studies used a comparison with normal saline which may underestimate
the effect of hypertonic saline.
•
A trial of nebulized hypertonic saline in an individual patient may aid in clearance of
secretions, but might not always be effective.
•
Health facilities experience overcrowding during peak RSV season, making any reduction
in the length of hospital stays through the use of nebulized hypertonic saline especially
valuable.
•
Health care workers should be aware that hypertonic saline can induce bronchoconstriction
and may often require co-administration with bronchodilators.

6.6.2 Justification
The recommendation for nebulized hypertonic saline was mainly driven by a modest reduction in the length
of stay among infants hospitalized with acute bronchiolitis, reduction in the risk of hospitalization among
outpatients and emergency department patients, and the earlier resolution of symptoms. A reduction of
hospital stay for even less than 1 day was considered clinically significant, particularly in RSV epidemic

inconsistency and risk of bias. In addition, nebulized hypertonic saline seems to be cost-effective and a
safe treatment in infants with bronchiolitis, with only minor and spontaneously resolved adverse events.
6.6.3 Summary of evidence
A systematic review identified 38 RCTs conducted over 20 years in Africa, the Americas, Asia and Europe,
involving a total of 5114 infants and children with bronchiolitis (208). Study sample sizes ranged from
40 to 772, and patients were followed up for 60 minutes to 72 hours for primary outcomes. All the trials
used nebulized hypertonic saline alone, or in conjunction with bronchodilators as an active intervention,
and nebulized normal (0.9%) saline or standard treatment as a comparator in children under 24 months
with acute bronchiolitis. The primary outcome was length of hospital stay for inpatient trials or rate of
hospitalization for the outpatient or emergency department trials. Other study outcomes included were
duration of oxygen therapy, resolution of symptoms and adverse events (208).
Hospitalized infants treated with nebulized hypertonic saline may have a shorter mean length of hospital
stay compared to those treated with nebulized normal (0.9%) saline or standard care (MD: −0.46 days; 95%
CI: −0.56 to −0.36, low-certainty evidence). The risk of hospitalization was reduced by 90 per 1000 patients
for nebulized hypertonic saline compared with nebulized normal saline plus salbutamol and was reduced
by 112 when compared to normal saline alone. Infants and children who received hypertonic saline had a
lower number of days to resolution of wheezing (lower by 1.16 days), cough (lower by 0.92 days), pulmonary
crackles (lower by 1.42 days) compared to those who received normal saline (low-certainty evidence). The
13 trials included in the review (2792 infants; 1479 treated with hypertonic saline, of which 416 (28%) were
co-administered with bronchodilators and 1063 (72%) treated with hypertonic saline alone) reported at
least one adverse event, such as worsening cough, agitation, bronchospasm, bradycardia, desaturation,
vomiting and diarrhoea, most of which were mild and resolved spontaneously (low-certainty evidence)
(208).
Overall, nebulized hypertonic saline may modestly reduce the length of stay among infants hospitalized
with acute bronchiolitis and may slightly reduce time to the resolution of symptoms. Treatment with
nebulized hypertonic saline may also reduce the risk of hospitalization among outpatients and emergency
department patients. Nebulized hypertonic saline seems to be a safe treatment in infants with bronchiolitis,
with only minor and spontaneously resolved adverse events. The certainty of the evidence was low to very
low for all outcomes, mainly due to inconsistency and risk of bias.
Benefits and desirable effects
The GDG judged the desirable effects of nebulized hypertonic (>3%) saline over 0.9% saline as moderate,
driven by a clinically significant reduction in length of hospital stay, and reduced hospitalization and
readmission rates. The GDG noted that that in peak RSV epidemic season, a reduction of hospital stay for
even less than 1 day should be considered significant, depending on the setting. In addition, nebulized
hypertonic saline contributed to earlier resolution of wheezing, cough and pulmonary crackles when
compared to the other interventions. The duration of oxygen therapy was also reduced by 15 minutes in
the hypertonic saline group, although this was not statistically significant.

# Annex 1. Management of


# conflicts of interest for the


# Guideline Development Group

In accordance with WHO policies and procedures for guideline development, all members of the Guideline
Development Group (GDG) were required to declare any potential conflicts of interest prior to their
involvement. This was done through the completion and electronic submission of the standard WHO
Declaration of Interests (DOI) form.
In addition, all GDG members, methodologists and external reviewers signed confidentiality agreements
to ensure the integrity and impartiality of the guideline development process.
All submitted DOI forms were reviewed by the WHO Secretariat. Any declared interests were assessed
and managed in line with the procedures outlined in the WHO handbook for guideline development6 and
WHO’s Declarations of interests for WHO experts.7 Where a declared interest was considered potentially
significant, appropriate measures were taken. These included conditional participation, partial exclusion
from specific discussions or full exclusion from the guideline process, depending on the nature and extent
of the interest.
At the beginning of each GDG meeting, participants were asked to declare any new or updated interests.
In cases where a GDG member had contributed to a study that informed the evidence for a specific
recommendation, that member was recused from the relevant discussions and decision-making, as
documented in the meeting records.
